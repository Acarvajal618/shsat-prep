Revising & Editing Study Guide

Every standalone (non-passage) Revising/Editing item transcribed so far from Practice Tests A–D, plus a full Kaplan drill workbook not tied to any specific exam — 58 real examples across 12 categories, grouped by the skill each one is actually testing.

How to use this page: don't try to memorize the rules first. Read a category's strategy box once, then go straight through all of its examples back to back — sentence, error, fix, one-line reason. The goal is to start recognizing the shape of each error type on sight, the same way you'd recognize a friend's face before you could describe their features in words. If an example makes sense on its own without you re-reading the strategy box, that's the pattern sinking in.

1Vague Pronoun Reference5 examples

A pronoun (he, she, it, they, this, that, these, those) has to clearly point back to one specific noun. If it could reasonably refer to two different things, or if there's no actual noun for it to point to at all (just an idea or situation), it's vague.

How to solve it: For every pronoun in the suspect sentence, ask "which exact noun does this replace?" If you can name two candidates, or if you can only describe a vague situation/idea rather than point to one noun, that's your answer. The fix is almost always to swap the pronoun for the actual name/noun.
Exam A · AR48
"Eliza and Brianna have been singing... Outside of school, she also sings in a choir..."
Why: "She" could mean Eliza or Brianna — the sentence never says which.
Workbook — Babe Ruth paragraph
"...hitting 20 home runs... was considered extraordinary, but that was drastically changed by Babe Ruth..."
Why: "That" has no single noun to point to — only a vague, unstated idea ("the perception that 20 HRs was a lot").
Workbook — Noctilucent Clouds passage
"Seeing these things in Europe has also happened more..." → "Sightings in Europe have also become more frequent..."
Why: "These things" is a lazy placeholder — swap it for the actual noun ("sightings") the sentence is really about.
Workbook — Icebreakers passage
"This process is time-consuming because it can be up to 16 feet thick." → "...because the ice can be up to 16 feet thick."
Why: "It" grammatically points back to "process" — but a process can't be "16 feet thick," only ice can. The pronoun is pointing at the wrong noun entirely.
Workbook — Elephant paragraph
"...both species live in herds... At 6.5 tons, this creature cannot easily escape..."
Why: The sentence right before talks about "both species" (plural) — "this creature" (singular) has no single elephant to refer back to.
2Subject–Verb, Pronoun-Antecedent & Tense Agreement6 examples

Three flavors of the same idea: a verb has to match its subject in number (singular ↔ singular), a pronoun has to match the noun it's standing in for in number, and every verb in a paragraph has to stay in the same tense unless there's a real reason to shift.

How to solve it: Find the actual subject of the verb — ignore any descriptive phrase sitting between the subject and the verb (that's the #1 trick these questions use). For pronouns, find the exact noun being replaced and check singular/plural match. For tense, read a sentence before and after and make sure the verb in question matches the surrounding tense.
Exam A · AR49
"Granddad turns each pancake when they start to bubble..." → "...when it starts to bubble..."
Why: The subject is singular ("each pancake"), so it needs "it starts," not "they start."
Exam B · BR09
"First she did vocal exercises... Then she studies the text..." → "Then she studied the text..."
Why: Every other verb in the paragraph is past tense. A present-tense verb breaks that consistency.
Workbook
"The most successful scientists, spanning fields from physics to geology, sees beyond the facts..." → "...see beyond the facts..."
Why: The subject is "scientists" (plural) — "spanning fields from physics to geology" is just a descriptive phrase sitting in between, not the real subject.
Workbook
"The Yankees is my favorite team because it always has amazing players..." → "The Yankees are my favorite team because they always have amazing players..."
Why: "The Yankees" (a team name ending in -s) is treated as plural throughout English, even though it names one team — every verb/pronoun referring to it needs to be plural too.
Workbook — Marie Curie paragraph
"Marie Curie, Pierre Curie, and Henri Becquerel were awarded... for work... that he had previously discovered."
Why: The subject is three people — "he" should be "they." Easy to miss because the pronoun is far from the list of names.
Workbook — Homeschooling paragraph
"Children of all ages enjoys the unlimited opportunities..." → "...enjoy the unlimited opportunities..."
Why: Plain plural subject, plain singular-verb mistake — the most basic version of this error, and still shows up.
3Sentence Construction (fragments, run-ons & dangling modifiers)6 examples

The single most common error type on this part of the exam. Three flavors: (a) a fragment — missing a subject or a complete verb, so it isn't actually a full sentence; (b) a run-on/comma splice — two complete sentences jammed together with just a comma; (c) a dangling/misplaced modifier — an opening descriptive phrase that ends up describing the wrong noun.

How to solve it: For fragments — does the sentence have both a subject AND a complete verb doing something? For run-ons — check whether the words on both sides of a comma could each stand alone as a full sentence; if yes, a comma alone isn't strong enough. For dangling modifiers — look at the noun immediately after the opening phrase and ask "does THIS actually do the action described?"
Exam B · BR06 — run-on
"...opened as part of the Smithsonian Institution, the museum is already the Smithsonian's third most popular site."
Why: Both halves are full sentences on their own — a comma can't join them.
Exam D · DR13 — dangling modifier
"Walking dogs, cleaning kennels, hand-feeding newborn kittens, the animal shelter is looking for volunteers..."
Why: A shelter (a place) can't walk dogs — the task list needs to modify a person ("volunteers"), not "the animal shelter."
Workbook — Title IX paragraph
"Resulted in more money in athletic departments allocated for women's sports..." (no subject at all)
Why: Fragment — "Resulted in..." never says WHAT resulted. There's no subject performing the action.
Workbook — California condor paragraph
"...this vulture can glide as far as 10 miles without flapping its wings, it conserves energy this way."
Why: Comma splice — two complete, independent ideas glued together with only a comma.
Workbook — Car wash sentence
"To raise money for uniforms, a car wash will be held by members of the volleyball team..." → "To raise money for uniforms, members of the volleyball team will hold a car wash..."
Why: "To raise money" needs to be followed by who's raising it — not "a car wash," which can't have a purpose of its own.
Workbook — Cathedral windows paragraph
"Light changes throughout the day and with the seasons, the windows' brightness can vary as a result."
Why: Another comma splice — two full sentences, one comma. Needs a period, semicolon, or a word like "so."
4Combining Sentences to Show Relationship5 examples

You're given 2–3 short separate sentences and asked which combined version best shows how the ideas relate — cause/effect, contrast, or one idea being extra detail about another.

How to solve it: First decide the real-world relationship in plain English ("this caused that," "these are just two facts," "this is extra detail about that"). Then eliminate any answer whose connector word (although, but, while, so, because) implies the wrong relationship — even if it reads smoothly.
Exam C · CR06
"Jordan rehearsed for weeks. He delivered his lines with ease." → "Because Jordan had rehearsed his role for weeks, he delivered his lines with ease..."
Why: "Because" correctly shows rehearsing caused the easy delivery — other options used "though"/"yet," wrongly implying the opposite.
Exam B · BR07
"Flyby missions... have happened since 1973. They let scientists collect data." → "Flyby missions..., which allow scientists to collect data..., have been happening since 1973."
Why: The data-collecting detail is background info, not an equally important separate fact — so it belongs in a "which" clause.
Workbook — Jean Piaget passage
"Piaget was born in Switzerland in 1896. He studied at the University of Neuchatel. He eventually received a doctorate..." → "Born in Switzerland in 1896, Piaget studied at the University of Neuchatel, where he eventually received a doctorate..."
Why: One clean sentence beats three choppy ones — "where" logically links the university to the doctorate earned there.
Workbook — Restaurant sentences
"The restaurant has had good business over the past two years. An increase in business has allowed the restaurant to expand." → "Because it has had good business over the past two years, the restaurant expanded."
Why: Good business caused the expansion — a trap answer reversed the cause and effect ("the restaurant expanded, increasing...business").
Workbook — Social Psychology passage
"The debate... is not new. The question was discussed as far back as ancient Greece." → "...is not new; the question was discussed as far back as ancient Greece."
Why: The second sentence just supports/elaborates the first — no contrast word needed, a plain semicolon is correct. Trap answers added "however" or "on the other hand," implying a contrast that isn't there.
5Punctuation Rules6 examples

Comma rules show up constantly: setting off an introductory phrase, setting off a nonessential/appositive phrase (needs commas on both sides), and adjective order — plus semicolons joining two complete ideas, and colons introducing a list.

How to solve it: Introductory phrase → comma right where the main sentence starts. Nonessential/appositive phrase (extra info you could delete and still have a complete sentence) → comma on both sides, not just one. Two complete sentences with no conjunction → semicolon, not a comma. A list after a complete sentence → colon.
Exam C · CR07
...delete the comma after "gelato" AND change "allow" to "allows" — a comma and an agreement fix bundled in one question.
Why: "Pair of revisions" questions often combine a punctuation fix with an agreement fix — check for both.
Workbook — Piano teacher sentence
"In constant demand as a piano teacher, my brother David has never been busier..."
Why: Introductory descriptive phrase — needs a comma right before the main subject ("my brother David") begins.
Workbook — Jemma sentence
"Unable to decide between a career in law and one in medicine, Jemma combined her two interests..."
Why: Same rule, longer introductory phrase — still just needs one comma where the main clause starts.
Workbook — Athletic complex sentence
"...features a myriad of amenities: multipurpose gymnasiums, an elevated jogging track..."
Why: A complete sentence followed by a list needs a colon to introduce it, not just a space or comma.
Workbook — Principal sentence
"Our principal, who met with students at the start of the school year, announced..."
Why: "Who met with students..." is nonessential extra info — it needs an opening comma to match the closing comma already there after "year."
Workbook — Miss Adams sentence
"Miss Adams, our swim team coach, chose Sarah to swim the final lap..."
Why: "Our swim team coach" is an appositive renaming "Miss Adams" — it opened with a comma but was missing its closing one.
6Precise / Effective Language5 examples

Two common traps here: (a) a vague phrase needs replacing with something concrete and specific, or (b) two near-synonyms are used together, which is just redundant ("difficult and challenging," "buy or purchase").

How to solve it: For vagueness — cross out any option that just rephrases the vague idea with another vague word; the right answer names the actual mechanism or specific detail. For redundancy — if two words in a row mean basically the same thing, the fix is almost always to cut one of them, not replace both.
Exam D · DR14
"...the tiled archways send the sound through the environment." → "...direct the sound waves to move along the curves of the arches."
Why: The fix names the actual physical cause instead of a vague restatement.
Workbook — Tennis sentence
"...too difficult and challenging to see the ball." → "...too difficult to see the ball."
Why: "Difficult" and "challenging" are near-synonyms — saying both is redundant.
Workbook — Sunscreen paragraph
"...80 percent of Americans buy or purchase sunscreen..." → "...80 percent of Americans buy sunscreen..."
Why: Same pattern — "buy" and "purchase" mean the same thing.
Workbook — Kaylee sentence
"...the plan Kaylee has is to attend college in Vermont..." → "...Kaylee plans to attend college in Vermont..."
Why: "The plan ___ has is to ___" is wordy and roundabout — a direct verb ("plans to") says the same thing more precisely.
Workbook — Democracy sentence
"...democracy's present-day and current principles..." → "...democracy's current principles..."
Why: "Present-day" and "current" are redundant synonyms — keep one.
7Transitions4 examples

You're given a blank at the start of a sentence and a word bank (e.g. "However," "For example," "Thus," "Moreover"). Pick the one that correctly signals how the new sentence relates to the one before it.

How to solve it: Name the relationship in plain English first — is the new sentence a contrast (however, nevertheless), a result (thus, consequently, therefore), a specific example (for example), or more of the same idea (moreover, in addition)? Only then look at the options.
Workbook — Social Psychology passage
"There is no question that humans have certain inborn behaviors... For example, infants instinctively grasp an object placed in their hands."
Why: The second sentence gives one specific instance of the general claim — a textbook example relationship.
Workbook — Social Psychology passage
Paragraph 1 describes an instinct-focused theory; paragraph 2 opens: "However, later scholars... attributed a significant percentage of human behavior to environmental influences."
Why: Two competing explanations for the same thing is a contrast — even without an obvious "opposite" word, infer it from the shift in whose theory is being described.
Workbook — Havana passage
"Trade from Havana was brisk... However, Spain lost control of Cuba in 1762..."
Why: A positive/established fact, then a negative turn — contrast, not a result ("therefore") or continuation ("consequently").
Workbook — Music Education paragraph
"We hoped our actions would make the school change its policy... However, we hoped in vain."
Why: "Hoped in vain" means the hope failed — a contrast with the hopeful tone just stated, not a result word like "thus" or "therefore."
8Organization / Sentence Order4 examples

A sentence is in the wrong spot — usually because it uses a word ("this," "there," "however," "instead") that only makes sense once some other sentence has already set it up.

How to solve it: Find the "hook" word in the sentence that needs to be moved (a pronoun, "there," "this," a term introduced via abbreviation) and locate exactly which other sentence supplies its missing context. The sentence has to move right next to that one — not just "somewhere earlier/later."
Workbook — Havana passage
Sentence 1 opens with "This lively center of all things Cuban..." before the city's name has been given. Sentence 6 names "Havana" explicitly.
Why: Sentence 6 should move to the very beginning — it's the only sentence that actually names Havana, so "This" has nothing to point back to until it moves there.
Workbook — Icebreakers passage
"The ice is instead crushed under the weight of the ship's bow..." needed to sit right after the sentence correcting the myth that icebreakers "push" the ice.
Why: "Instead" only makes sense immediately after the myth it's correcting.
Workbook — Jean Piaget passage
"There, Piaget applied his knowledge of the environment's effect on biological change" needed to sit right after the sentence naming "the Sorbonne in Paris."
Why: "There" is a placeholder for a location — it has to immediately follow the sentence that actually names the place.
Workbook — Nizhny Novgorod passage
"It was the art, however, that generated a storm of controversy..." was misplaced relative to the sentence describing the two competing art styles being contrasted.
Why: "However" signals a contrast with something specific just said — the sentence has to sit right after that specific thing.
9Off-Topic Sentence Removal4 examples

One sentence in a paragraph brings up something technically related to the subject but not to the specific point the paragraph is actually making.

How to solve it: State the paragraph's main point in one sentence of your own. Then check each option against that — "does this directly support or explain my one-sentence summary?" The trap sentence is usually true and loosely on-subject, just not relevant to the specific argument being built.
Workbook — Havana passage
A paragraph about shifts in political control over Havana/Cuba includes a sentence about Che Guevara's personal background (doctor, writer).
Why: Related to the same time period, but a biographical tangent, not part of the "who controlled Havana" throughline.
Workbook — Jean Piaget passage
A paragraph about Piaget's own career includes "B. F. Skinner and Albert Bandura also studied child development."
Why: True and related to psychology in general, but it shifts focus to other researchers, off the paragraph's actual topic (Piaget specifically).
Workbook — Icebreakers passage
A paragraph about early ship designs adapting to ice includes "The winter of 1880–1881 also froze the Elbe river in Germany."
Why: A standalone fact about weather, with no connection to ship-design technology — unlike every other sentence in that paragraph.
Workbook — Crisis Management passage
A paragraph about Odwalla's crisis response includes "Odwalla also makes fruit and cereal bars."
Why: True and about Odwalla, but the paragraph is specifically about their crisis response — what else they manufacture doesn't belong.
10Best Concluding / Supporting Sentence4 examples

Pick which sentence should be added to finish a paragraph, or to support/set up a claim just made. The wrong answers are usually too narrow (one more small detail) or contradict what the passage actually argues.

How to solve it: A real conclusion ties together everything the paragraph/passage already said — it shouldn't introduce a brand-new, unsupported idea. A real supporting sentence directly sets up the logic of the sentence that follows it. Eliminate anything that just restates the one sentence right before it, contradicts the passage, or brings up a topic never discussed elsewhere.
Workbook — Winter essay
After three paragraphs each giving one reason winter is the writer's favorite season: "For all of these reasons, winter is the season of the year that I enjoy the most."
Why: It explicitly ties together "all of these reasons" — directly restating the passage's thesis.
Workbook — Crisis Management passage
"A company can easily make a good decision once an emergency is over." → best next sentence: "The real challenge is making them in times of anxiety and panic."
Why: Supplies the missing contrast (easy in hindsight vs. hard in the moment) that logically sets up the next sentence.
Workbook — Icebreakers passage
"Rather, their ability to break through thick ice is due to several unique design features..." → best sentence to come before it: "Icebreakers do not have an immense weight that breaks the ice."
Why: "Rather" signals a correction of a prior claim — the supporting sentence has to set up exactly that misconception for it to correct.
Workbook — Social Psychology passage
Final sentence after a paragraph covering case histories, surveys, and experiments as research methods: "Social psychologists use case histories, surveys, and experiments to study human behavior."
Why: It's a clean summary of exactly what the paragraph just covered — not a new tangent and not just restating the sentence right before it.
11Style & Tone Matching4 examples

A sentence is too casual (or too flowery) for the rest of the passage. You're asked which revision best matches the register already established — not just which one sounds "more fancy" or "more correct" in isolation.

How to solve it: Read a neighboring sentence to confirm the passage's actual register (plain/factual vs. literary vs. first-person narrative). Cross out any option that's informal/conversational ("you may know," "stuff," "super quick") and any option that overcorrects into purple, exaggerated prose. The right answer is usually the plainest, most direct version that keeps all the original facts and matches the passage's point of view (first person vs. third person).
Workbook — Noctilucent Clouds passage
"We generally agree that a change in atmospheric phenomena..." → "Scientists generally agree that a change in atmospheric phenomena..."
Why: Swaps the vague "We" for the objective, specific "Scientists" — matching the rest of the passage's factual register.
Workbook — Cotton gin / Eli Whitney passage
"...took the fibers people actually wanted..." → "...efficiently caught the desired fibers..."
Why: The casual aside doesn't fit a passage otherwise written in a historical/technical register.
Workbook — Social Psychology passage
"The field should expand even further." → "In recent years, the field of social psychology has expanded even further."
Why: The next sentences describe what social psychologists "today also study" — an already-expanded current practice, not a future hope. Match the verb tense to the content, not just the formality.
Workbook — Music Education paragraph
"Those comments made them feel that we were really effecting our school atmosphere." → "...made us feel that we were really affecting our school atmosphere."
Why: The passage is first-person throughout ("My friends and I") so "us" is correct, not "them" — and "affecting" (influencing) is the right word, not "effecting" (bringing about). Style matching isn't just about formality — it includes point of view and word choice consistency too.
12Commonly Confused Words & Word Forms5 examples

A grab-bag of small, specific mix-ups that show up again and again: possessive vs. contraction ("its" vs. "it's"), "who" vs. "that" for people, irregular past participles ("rang" vs. "rung"), and comparative vs. superlative ("better" vs. "best").

How to solve it: These aren't really about logic — they're about recognizing five or six specific, recurring mix-ups. its/it's: "it's" always means "it is" — if you can't substitute "it is" into the sentence, it should be "its." who/that: use "who" for people, "that" for things. Comparative vs. superlative: comparing exactly 2 things → "-er"/"more" (better, more); comparing 3+ things → "-est"/"most" (best, most). Irregular verbs: past tense ("rang," "swam," "began") vs. the form used with "has/have/was/were" (rung, swum, begun) are NOT interchangeable.
Workbook — Statue sentence
"...it's presence is now dominated by the enormous skyscrapers..." → "...its presence is now dominated..."
Why: Try the substitution: "it is presence" doesn't make sense, so it needs the possessive "its" (no apostrophe).
Workbook — Sprinter sentence
"Between Hugo and Kai, Hugo is the best sprinter..." → "...the better sprinter..."
Why: Only two people are being compared ("Between Hugo and Kai") — that calls for the comparative "better," not the superlative "best" (reserved for 3+).
Workbook — Dog show paragraph
"...offers scholarships to the brighter veterinary students." → "...to the brightest veterinary students."
Why: The opposite mistake — selecting among many students (3+) needs the superlative "brightest," not the comparative "brighter."
Workbook — Liberty Bell paragraph
"...an enormous instrument that was eventually rang during the American Revolution..." → "...was eventually rung..."
Why: After "was/were" (passive voice), irregular verbs need their participle form — "rung," not the simple past "rang" (you'd say "it rang" but "it was rung").
Workbook — Women's equality paragraph
"...young women that did not previously consider a career in business..." → "...young women who did not previously consider..."
Why: "Women" are people — use "who," not "that" (which is for things/objects/ideas).

Strategy, top to bottom: read the sentence(s) once for meaning before touching the error. Identify which of the 12 skills above is being tested from the question phrasing. Then check only for that error — don't go hunting for unrelated problems. This guide grows as more material gets transcribed.
