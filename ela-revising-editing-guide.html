Revising & Editing Study Guide

Every standalone (non-passage) Revising/Editing item transcribed so far from Practice Tests A–D, plus a general Kaplan drill workbook not tied to any specific exam — grouped by the skill each one is actually testing. 11 categories now, spanning everything from single-sentence grammar fixes to paragraph-level organization.

1Vague Pronoun Reference1 example · AR48

A pronoun (he, she, it, they, this) has to clearly point back to one specific noun. If it could reasonably refer to two different people or things mentioned earlier, it's vague.

How to solve it: For every pronoun in the suspect sentence, ask "which exact noun does this replace?" If you can name two candidates, or if the real antecedent was never stated, that's your answer. The fix is always to swap the pronoun for the actual name/noun.
Exam A · AR48
"Eliza and Brianna have been singing... Outside of school, she also sings in a choir..."
Why: "She" could mean Eliza or Brianna — the sentence never says which. Fix: name her directly (Eliza or Brianna).
2Subject–Verb & Tense Agreement2 examples · AR49, BR09

The verb has to match its subject in number (singular subject → singular verb), and every verb in a paragraph has to stay in the same tense unless there's a real reason to shift.

How to solve it: Find the actual subject of the verb — ignore any phrase sitting between them. Then check what tense the rest of the paragraph is written in (read a sentence before and after) and make sure the verb in question matches it.
Exam A · AR49
"Granddad turns each pancake when they start to bubble..." → "...when it starts to bubble..."
Why: The subject is singular ("each pancake"), so it needs the singular pronoun/verb "it starts," not "they start."
Exam B · BR09
"First she did vocal exercises... Then she studies the text..." → "Then she studied the text..."
Why: Every other verb in the paragraph is past tense ("did," "spent"). A present-tense verb ("studies") breaks that consistency.
3Sentence Construction (run-ons & dangling modifiers)5 examples · AR50, BR06, BR08, CR05, DR13

The most common error type on this part of the exam. Two flavors show up over and over: (a) a run-on/comma splice — two complete sentences jammed together with just a comma — and (b) a dangling/misplaced modifier — an opening descriptive phrase that ends up describing the wrong noun.

How to solve it: For run-ons — check whether the words on both sides of a comma could each stand alone as a full sentence. If yes, a comma alone isn't strong enough; it needs a period, semicolon, or conjunction. For dangling modifiers — look at the noun immediately after the opening phrase and ask "does THIS actually do the action described?" If not, it's dangling.
Exam B · BR06 — comma splice
"...opened as part of the Smithsonian Institution, the museum is already the Smithsonian's third most popular site."
Why: Both halves are full sentences on their own, so a comma can't join them — needs a period or a conjunction.
Exam B · BR08 — dangling modifier
"Leaving approximately 73,000 residents without electricity, lights did not function, refrigerators did not stay cold..."
Why: The phrase describes the power outage "leaving" residents without power — but the noun right after the comma is "lights," which can't "leave" anyone without electricity.
Exam D · DR13 — dangling modifier
"Walking dogs, cleaning kennels, hand-feeding newborn kittens, the animal shelter is looking for volunteers..."
Why: A shelter (a place) can't walk dogs or feed kittens — the list of tasks needs to modify a person ("volunteers"), not "the animal shelter."
4Combining Sentences to Show Relationship3 examples · BR07, CR06, DR16

You're given 2–3 short separate sentences and asked which combined version best shows how the ideas relate — cause/effect, contrast, or one idea being extra detail about another.

How to solve it: First decide the real-world relationship in plain English ("this caused that," "these are just two facts," "this is an extra detail about that"). Then eliminate any answer whose connector word (although, but, while, so, because) implies the wrong relationship — even if the sentence reads smoothly. The most important idea should end up as the main clause, not buried in a subordinate phrase.
Exam C · CR06
"Jordan rehearsed for weeks. He delivered his lines with ease. His performance got applause." → "Because Jordan had rehearsed his role for weeks, he delivered his lines with ease, and his performance was met with applause."
Why: "Because" correctly shows rehearsing caused the easy delivery — other options used "though"/"yet," which wrongly imply the opposite (success despite rehearsing).
Exam B · BR07
"Flyby missions near Jupiter have happened since 1973. They let scientists collect data." → "Flyby missions near Jupiter, which allow scientists to collect data about the planet and its moons, have been happening since 1973."
Why: The data-collecting detail is background info about the missions, not an equally important separate fact — so it belongs in a "which" clause, not its own independent clause.
5Punctuation Rules (commas & adjective order)2 examples · CR07, DR15

Mostly comma rules: whether two adjectives before a noun need a comma between them ("coordinate" adjectives) and whether a descriptive clause is essential (no commas) or extra/nonessential (needs commas).

How to solve it — the "AND test" for adjective commas: Try putting "and" between the two adjectives, or swapping their order. If it still sounds natural ("official and first selfie"), they're coordinate and need a comma. If it sounds wrong, no comma — they're not independently describing the noun.
Exam D · DR15
"...taking the first, official selfie..." → "...taking the first official selfie..."
Why: "First official selfie" isn't "first AND official" selfie — he didn't take two separate things. The adjectives build on each other, so no comma.
Exam C · CR07
Paragraph-editing item: pick the sentence needing BOTH a comma fix and an agreement fix in the same pass (delete comma after "gelato" AND change "allow" → "allows").
Why: "Pair of revisions" questions often bundle a punctuation fix with an agreement fix in the same sentence — check both, not just one.
6Precise / Effective Language1 example · DR14

You're asked to replace a vague phrase with the most precise option. The trap answers just use different generic words that are still vague — the correct one adds concrete, specific detail.

How to solve it: Cross out any option that merely rephrases the vague idea with another vague word ("a specific direction," "through the space"). The right answer explains the actual mechanism — what specifically happens, and how.
Exam D · DR14
"...the tiled archways send the sound through the environment." → "...the tiled archways direct the sound waves to move along the curves of the arches."
Why: The fix names the actual physical cause (sound waves following curved tile) instead of a vague restatement of "send it somewhere."
7TransitionsWorkbook drill set

You're given a blank at the start of a sentence and a word bank (e.g. "However," "For example," "Thus," "Nevertheless,"). Pick the one that correctly signals how the new sentence relates to the one before it.

How to solve it: Name the relationship in plain English first — is the new sentence a contrast (however, nevertheless), a result (thus, consequently), a specific example (for example), or more of the same idea (moreover, similarly)? Only then look at the options — most wrong answers are contrast words used where the real relationship is support or result, or vice versa.
Workbook — Social Psychology passage
"There is no question that humans have certain inborn behaviors, or instincts. For example, infants instinctively grasp an object placed in their hands."
Why: The second sentence gives one specific instance of the general claim just made — a textbook example relationship, not a contrast or result.
Workbook — Social Psychology passage
Paragraph 1 describes an instinct-focused theory; paragraph 2 opens with later scholars attributing behavior to environment instead. However, later scholars...
Why: Two competing explanations for the same phenomenon is a contrast, even though no single word in the sentence screams "opposite" — you have to infer it from the shift in whose theory is being described.
8Organization / Sentence OrderWorkbook drill set

A sentence is in the wrong spot — usually because it uses a word ("this," "there," "however") that only makes sense once some other sentence has already set it up.

How to solve it: Find the "hook" word in the sentence that needs to be moved (a pronoun, "there," "this," a term introduced via abbreviation) and locate exactly which other sentence supplies its missing context. The sentence has to move next to that one — not just "somewhere earlier/later."
Workbook — Havana passage
Sentence 1 opens with "This lively center of all things Cuban..." before the city's name has been given. Sentence 6, at the end of the paragraph, says "The city of Havana stands today as a testament to its turbulent yet glorious history."
Why: Sentence 6 should move to the very beginning — it's the only sentence that actually names Havana, so "This" in the original sentence 1 has nothing to point back to until it moves.
Workbook — Icebreakers passage
"The ice is instead crushed under the weight of the ship's bow..." was placed before the sentence explaining that icebreakers do not "push" the ice despite appearances.
Why: "Instead" only makes sense immediately after the myth it's correcting — move the sentence right after that one.
9Off-Topic Sentence RemovalWorkbook drill set

One sentence in a paragraph brings up something technically related to the subject but not to the specific point the paragraph is actually making.

How to solve it: State the paragraph's main point in one sentence of your own. Then check each option against that one sentence — "does this directly support or explain my one-sentence summary?" The trap sentence is usually true and on-subject in a loose sense, just not relevant to the specific argument being built.
Workbook — Crisis Management passage
A paragraph about Odwalla's E. coli crisis response includes: "Odwalla also makes fruit and cereal bars."
Why: True and about Odwalla, but the paragraph is specifically about their crisis response (recall, press releases, safety improvements) — what else they manufacture doesn't belong.
Workbook — Havana passage
A paragraph about shifts in political control over Havana/Cuba includes a sentence about Che Guevara's personal background (doctor, writer) rather than the control/governance thread the rest of the paragraph follows.
Why: Related to the same historical period, but a biographical tangent rather than part of the "who controlled Havana" throughline.
10Best Concluding / Supporting SentenceWorkbook drill set

Pick which sentence should be added to finish a paragraph or support a claim. The wrong answers are usually either too narrow (just one more small detail) or they contradict what the passage actually argues.

How to solve it: A real conclusion ties together everything the paragraph/passage already said — it shouldn't introduce a brand-new, unsupported idea. Eliminate anything that (a) only restates the single sentence right before it, (b) contradicts the passage's own argument, or (c) brings up a topic never discussed elsewhere in the passage.
Workbook — Winter essay
After three paragraphs each giving one reason winter is the writer's favorite season: "For all of these reasons, winter is the season of the year that I enjoy the most."
Why: It explicitly ties together "all of these reasons" — directly restating the passage's thesis — while the other options were narrow supporting details or a tangent.
Workbook — Crisis Management passage
"A company can easily make a good decision once an emergency is over." → best next sentence: "The real challenge is making them in times of anxiety and panic."
Why: It supplies the missing contrast (easy in hindsight vs. hard in the moment) that the next sentence's "therefore" then logically builds on.
11Style & Tone MatchingWorkbook drill set

A sentence is too casual (or too flowery) for the rest of the passage. You're asked which revision best matches the register already established — not just which one is "more fancy."

How to solve it: Read a neighboring sentence to confirm the passage's actual register (plain/factual vs. literary). Cross out any option that's informal/conversational ("you may know," "stuff," "super quick") and any option that overcorrects into purple, exaggerated prose ("sensational," "presage imminent"). The right answer is usually the plainest, most direct version that keeps all the original facts.
Workbook — Noctilucent Clouds passage
"We generally agree that a change in atmospheric phenomena..." → "Scientists generally agree that a change in atmospheric phenomena..."
Why: Swaps the vague "We" for the objective, specific "Scientists" — matching the rest of the passage's factual register, without sliding into overwritten vocabulary like "sensational transformation."
Workbook — Cotton gin / Eli Whitney passage
"...took the fibers people actually wanted..." → "...efficiently caught the desired fibers..."
Why: The casual aside "people actually wanted" doesn't fit a passage otherwise written in a historical/technical register.

Strategy, top to bottom: read the sentence(s) once for meaning before touching the error. Identify which of the 11 skills above is being tested from the question phrasing. Then check only for that error — don't go hunting for unrelated problems. This guide grows as more material gets transcribed.
