Revising & Editing Study Guide

Every standalone (non-passage) Revising/Editing item transcribed so far from Practice Tests A–D — 14 real questions, grouped by the skill each one is actually testing. There aren't enough yet for a full practice set, but they already span the 6 error types that show up again and again on this part of the exam.

1Vague Pronoun Reference1 example · AR48

A pronoun (he, she, it, they, this) has to clearly point back to one specific noun. If it could reasonably refer to two different people or things mentioned earlier, it's vague.

How to solve it: For every pronoun in the suspect sentence, ask "which exact noun does this replace?" If you can name two candidates, or if the real antecedent was never stated, that's your answer. The fix is always to swap the pronoun for the actual name/noun.
Exam A · AR48
"Eliza and Brianna have been singing... Outside of school, she also sings in a choir..."
Why: "She" could mean Eliza or Brianna — the sentence never says which. Fix: name her directly (Eliza or Brianna).
2Subject–Verb & Tense Agreement2 examples · AR49, BR09

The verb has to match its subject in number (singular subject → singular verb), and every verb in a paragraph has to stay in the same tense unless there's a real reason to shift.

How to solve it: Find the actual subject of the verb — ignore any phrase sitting between them. Then check what tense the rest of the paragraph is written in (read a sentence before and after) and make sure the verb in question matches it.
Exam A · AR49
"Granddad turns each pancake when they start to bubble..." → "...when it starts to bubble..."
Why: The subject is singular ("each pancake"), so it needs the singular pronoun/verb "it starts," not "they start."
Exam B · BR09
"First she did vocal exercises... Then she studies the text..." → "Then she studied the text..."
Why: Every other verb in the paragraph is past tense ("did," "spent"). A present-tense verb ("studies") breaks that consistency.
3Sentence Construction (run-ons & dangling modifiers)5 examples · AR50, BR06, BR08, CR05, DR13

The most common error type on this part of the exam. Two flavors show up over and over: (a) a run-on/comma splice — two complete sentences jammed together with just a comma — and (b) a dangling/misplaced modifier — an opening descriptive phrase that ends up describing the wrong noun.

How to solve it: For run-ons — check whether the words on both sides of a comma could each stand alone as a full sentence. If yes, a comma alone isn't strong enough; it needs a period, semicolon, or conjunction. For dangling modifiers — look at the noun immediately after the opening phrase and ask "does THIS actually do the action described?" If not, it's dangling.
Exam B · BR06 — comma splice
"...opened as part of the Smithsonian Institution, the museum is already the Smithsonian's third most popular site."
Why: Both halves are full sentences on their own, so a comma can't join them — needs a period or a conjunction.
Exam B · BR08 — dangling modifier
"Leaving approximately 73,000 residents without electricity, lights did not function, refrigerators did not stay cold..."
Why: The phrase describes the power outage "leaving" residents without power — but the noun right after the comma is "lights," which can't "leave" anyone without electricity.
Exam D · DR13 — dangling modifier
"Walking dogs, cleaning kennels, hand-feeding newborn kittens, the animal shelter is looking for volunteers..."
Why: A shelter (a place) can't walk dogs or feed kittens — the list of tasks needs to modify a person ("volunteers"), not "the animal shelter."
4Combining Sentences to Show Relationship3 examples · BR07, CR06, DR16

You're given 2–3 short separate sentences and asked which combined version best shows how the ideas relate — cause/effect, contrast, or one idea being extra detail about another.

How to solve it: First decide the real-world relationship in plain English ("this caused that," "these are just two facts," "this is an extra detail about that"). Then eliminate any answer whose connector word (although, but, while, so, because) implies the wrong relationship — even if the sentence reads smoothly. The most important idea should end up as the main clause, not buried in a subordinate phrase.
Exam C · CR06
"Jordan rehearsed for weeks. He delivered his lines with ease. His performance got applause." → "Because Jordan had rehearsed his role for weeks, he delivered his lines with ease, and his performance was met with applause."
Why: "Because" correctly shows rehearsing caused the easy delivery — other options used "though"/"yet," which wrongly imply the opposite (success despite rehearsing).
Exam B · BR07
"Flyby missions near Jupiter have happened since 1973. They let scientists collect data." → "Flyby missions near Jupiter, which allow scientists to collect data about the planet and its moons, have been happening since 1973."
Why: The data-collecting detail is background info about the missions, not an equally important separate fact — so it belongs in a "which" clause, not its own independent clause.
5Punctuation Rules (commas & adjective order)2 examples · CR07, DR15

Mostly comma rules: whether two adjectives before a noun need a comma between them ("coordinate" adjectives) and whether a descriptive clause is essential (no commas) or extra/nonessential (needs commas).

How to solve it — the "AND test" for adjective commas: Try putting "and" between the two adjectives, or swapping their order. If it still sounds natural ("official and first selfie"), they're coordinate and need a comma. If it sounds wrong, no comma — they're not independently describing the noun.
Exam D · DR15
"...taking the first, official selfie..." → "...taking the first official selfie..."
Why: "First official selfie" isn't "first AND official" selfie — he didn't take two separate things. The adjectives build on each other, so no comma.
Exam C · CR07
Paragraph-editing item: pick the sentence needing BOTH a comma fix and an agreement fix in the same pass (delete comma after "gelato" AND change "allow" → "allows").
Why: "Pair of revisions" questions often bundle a punctuation fix with an agreement fix in the same sentence — check both, not just one.
6Precise / Effective Language1 example · DR14

You're asked to replace a vague phrase with the most precise option. The trap answers just use different generic words that are still vague — the correct one adds concrete, specific detail.

How to solve it: Cross out any option that merely rephrases the vague idea with another vague word ("a specific direction," "through the space"). The right answer explains the actual mechanism — what specifically happens, and how.
Exam D · DR14
"...the tiled archways send the sound through the environment." → "...the tiled archways direct the sound waves to move along the curves of the arches."
Why: The fix names the actual physical cause (sound waves following curved tile) instead of a vague restatement of "send it somewhere."

Strategy, top to bottom: read the sentence(s) once for meaning before touching the error. Identify which of the 6 skills above is being tested from the question phrasing. Then check only for that error — don't go hunting for unrelated problems. As more standalone items get transcribed from Exams A–D, this guide will grow with them.
