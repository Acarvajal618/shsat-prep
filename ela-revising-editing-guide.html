Revising & Editing Study Guide

Every standalone (non-passage) Revising/Editing item transcribed so far from Practice Tests A–D, plus a full Kaplan drill workbook not tied to any specific exam — 59 real examples across 12 categories. Every example shows the complete original sentence or paragraph, not a trimmed fragment, so you can see the error sitting in its real context the way it'll actually appear on the test.

How to use this page: don't try to memorize the rules first. Read a category's strategy box once, then go straight through all of its examples back to back — sentence, error, fix, one-line reason. The goal is to start recognizing the shape of each error type on sight, the same way you'd recognize a friend's face before you could describe their features in words. If an example makes sense on its own without you re-reading the strategy box, that's the pattern sinking in.

1Vague Pronoun Reference5 examples

A pronoun (he, she, it, they, this, that, these, those) has to clearly point back to one specific noun. If it could reasonably refer to two different things, or if there's no actual noun for it to point to at all (just an idea or situation), it's vague.

How to solve it: For every pronoun in the suspect sentence, ask "which exact noun does this replace?" If you can name two candidates, or if you can only describe a vague situation/idea rather than point to one noun, that's your answer. The fix is almost always to swap the pronoun for the actual name/noun.
Exam A · AR48
(1) Eliza and Brianna have been singing in their school chorus since they were in fourth grade. (2) The girls always sing a duet at the school talent show, and they take turns singing the national anthem before school sporting events. (3) Outside of school, she also sings in a choir made up of young and old members of her community. (4) Both girls hope that they will be able to continue singing for many more years.
Why: "She" in sentence 3 could mean Eliza or Brianna — nothing in the paragraph says which one sings in the community choir.
Workbook — Babe Ruth paragraph
(1) Babe Ruth started his career as a pitcher for the Boston Red Sox and then joined the New York Yankees. (2) In baseball's early days, hitting 20 home runs in a season was considered extraordinary, but that was drastically changed by Babe Ruth when he hit 54 home runs in 1920. (3) When he retired in 1935, he had made a total of 714 home runs. (4) Two movies about Babe Ruth feature his accomplishments...
Why: "That" in sentence 2 has no single noun to point to — only a vague, unstated idea ("the perception that 20 home runs was extraordinary").
Workbook — Noctilucent Clouds passage
"Seeing these things in Europe has also happened more and more in the past 50 years, for reasons that remain unsure." → "Sightings in Europe have also become more frequent in the past 50 years, for reasons that remain unclear."
Why: "These things" is a lazy placeholder — swap it for the actual noun ("sightings") the sentence is really about.
Workbook — Icebreakers passage
"This process is time-consuming because it can be up to 16 feet thick." → "This process is time-consuming because the ice can be up to 16 feet thick."
Why: "It" grammatically points back to "process" — but a process can't be "16 feet thick," only ice can. The pronoun is pointing at the wrong noun entirely.
Workbook — Elephant paragraph
(1) The African elephant, having much larger ears, is slightly heavier than the Indian elephant. (2) In their respective regions, both species live in herds and have a vegetarian diet. (3) At 6.5 tons, this creature cannot easily escape from people, who have tamed elephants for various reasons. (4) The African elephant was long considered one of the most endangered creatures...
Why: Sentence 2 just said "both species" (plural) — "this creature" (singular) in sentence 3 has no single elephant to refer back to.
2Subject–Verb, Pronoun-Antecedent & Tense Agreement6 examples

Three flavors of the same idea: a verb has to match its subject in number (singular ↔ singular), a pronoun has to match the noun it's standing in for in number, and every verb in a paragraph has to stay in the same tense unless there's a real reason to shift.

How to solve it: Find the actual subject of the verb — ignore any descriptive phrase sitting between the subject and the verb (that's the #1 trick these questions use). For pronouns, find the exact noun being replaced and check singular/plural match. For tense, read a sentence before and after and make sure the verb in question matches the surrounding tense.
Exam A · AR49
"Granddad turns each pancake when they start to bubble, while all three siblings get the table ready for a sweet delicious breakfast." → "...when it starts to bubble..."
Why: The subject is singular ("each pancake"), so it needs "it starts," not "they start."
Exam B · BR09
"(1) Danielle spent several hours preparing for an upcoming audition... (2) First she did vocal exercises... (3) Then she studies the text of the monologue... (4) Finally she recited her monologue..."
Why: Every other verb in the paragraph is past tense ("spent," "did," "recited"). Sentence 3's present-tense "studies" breaks that consistency — should be "studied."
Workbook
"The most successful scientists, spanning fields from physics to geology, sees beyond the facts and speculate about the general principles that provide a foundation for new ideas." → "...see beyond the facts..."
Why: The subject is "scientists" (plural) — "spanning fields from physics to geology" is just a descriptive phrase sitting in between, not the real subject.
Workbook
"The Yankees is my favorite team because it always has amazing players and has an impressive history." → "The Yankees are my favorite team because they always have amazing players and have an impressive history."
Why: "The Yankees" (a team name ending in -s) is treated as plural throughout English, even though it names one team — every verb/pronoun referring to it needs to be plural too.
Workbook — Marie Curie paragraph
(1) Marie Curie, Pierre Curie, and Henri Becquerel were awarded the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics for work studying radiation phenomena that he had previously discovered. (2) After her husband, Pierre, passed away, Marie studied the medical applications of radium... (3) The Curie family included many scientists... (4) In fact, their daughter, Irene, followed in her parents' footsteps...
Why: The subject of sentence 1 is three people — "he" should be "they." Easy to miss because the pronoun is far from the list of names.
Workbook — Homeschooling paragraph
(1) Homeschooling programs, nearly unheard of 20 years ago, are now available through many public school systems. (2) Children of all ages enjoys the unlimited opportunities and a variety of tailored options. (3) Many homeschool families choose an eclectic approach... (4) High school students receive a transcript, which most universities accept.
Why: Plain plural subject ("Children"), plain singular-verb mistake ("enjoys") — the most basic version of this error, and it still shows up.
3Sentence Construction (fragments, run-ons & dangling modifiers)6 examples

The single most common error type on this part of the exam. Three flavors: (a) a fragment — missing a subject or a complete verb, so it isn't actually a full sentence; (b) a run-on/comma splice — two complete sentences jammed together with just a comma; (c) a dangling/misplaced modifier — an opening descriptive phrase that ends up describing the wrong noun.

How to solve it: For fragments — does the sentence have both a subject AND a complete verb doing something? For run-ons — check whether the words on both sides of a comma could each stand alone as a full sentence; if yes, a comma alone isn't strong enough. For dangling modifiers — look at the noun immediately after the opening phrase and ask "does THIS actually do the action described?"
Exam B · BR06 — run-on
"In September 2016 the National Museum of African American History and Culture opened as part of the Smithsonian Institution, the museum is already the Smithsonian's third most popular site."
Why: Both halves are full sentences on their own — a comma can't join them.
Exam D · DR13 — dangling modifier
(1) Walking dogs, cleaning kennels, hand-feeding newborn kittens, and supporting the pet-adoption process, the animal shelter is looking for volunteers to help with a variety of tasks. (2) Working at the animal shelter is a great way for young people... (3) In addition to hands-on training... (4) Caring for animals can also help volunteers develop empathy...
Why: A shelter (a place) can't walk dogs — the task list needs to modify a person ("volunteers"), not "the animal shelter."
Workbook — Title IX paragraph
(1) Colleges could give greater support to men's sports teams than to women's teams prior to 1972; today, women's and men's teams must receive equal funding. (2) Title IX of the Education Amendments prohibits gender discrimination at colleges and other federally funded institutions. (3) Resulted in more money in athletic departments allocated for women's sports, greater competition among teams, and improvements in the quality of athletics for women. (4) Title IX has led to more interest in women's sports.
Why: Fragment — sentence 3 never says WHAT resulted in that money. There's no subject performing the action.
Workbook — California condor paragraph
(1) Since only 17 of them were living in 1986, the California condor faced extinction, but the population has since flourished and increased to more than 350 in the wild. (2) Despite weighing at least 25 pounds, this vulture can glide as far as 10 miles without ever flapping its wings. (3) It feeds on large carcasses, the bird does not attack any living creatures. (4) To successfully protect them from extinction, concerned groups took the California condors...
Why: Comma splice — "It feeds on large carcasses" and "the bird does not attack any living creatures" are both complete, independent ideas glued together with only a comma.
Workbook — Car wash sentence
"To raise money for uniforms, a car wash will be held by members of the volleyball team on Saturday." → "To raise money for uniforms, members of the volleyball team will hold a car wash on Saturday."
Why: "To raise money" needs to be followed by who's raising it — not "a car wash," which can't have a purpose of its own.
Workbook — Cathedral windows paragraph
(1) Cathedral windows are often intricate, composed of thousands of pieces of luminous stained glass. (2) Light changes throughout the day and with the seasons, the windows' brightness can vary as a result. (3) Starting in the twelfth century, European churches installed beautiful stained glass windows. (4) In the nineteenth-century, interest in this glass renewed...
Why: Another comma splice — two full sentences, one comma. Needs a period, semicolon, or a word like "so."
4Combining Sentences to Show Relationship5 examples

You're given 2–3 short separate sentences and asked which combined version best shows how the ideas relate — cause/effect, contrast, or one idea being extra detail about another.

How to solve it: First decide the real-world relationship in plain English ("this caused that," "these are just two facts," "this is extra detail about that"). Then eliminate any answer whose connector word (although, but, while, so, because) implies the wrong relationship — even if it reads smoothly.
Exam C · CR06
"Jordan rehearsed his role for weeks. He delivered his lines with ease. His performance was met with a burst of applause from the audience." → "Because Jordan had rehearsed his role for weeks, he delivered his lines with ease, and his performance was met with a burst of applause from the audience."
Why: "Because" correctly shows rehearsing caused the easy delivery — other options used "though"/"yet," wrongly implying the opposite.
Exam B · BR07
"(1) Flyby missions near Jupiter have been happening since 1973. (2) Flyby missions allow scientists to collect data about Jupiter and its moons." → "Flyby missions near Jupiter, which allow scientists to collect data about the planet and its moons, have been happening since 1973."
Why: The data-collecting detail is background info, not an equally important separate fact — so it belongs in a "which" clause.
Workbook — Jean Piaget passage
"(2) Piaget was born in Switzerland in 1896. (3) He studied at the University of Neuchatel. (4) He eventually received a doctorate in the natural sciences." → "Born in Switzerland in 1896, Piaget studied at the University of Neuchatel, where he eventually received a doctorate in the natural sciences."
Why: One clean sentence beats three choppy ones — "where" logically links the university to the doctorate earned there.
Workbook — Restaurant sentences
"(1) The restaurant has had good business over the past two years. (2) An increase in business has allowed the restaurant to expand." → "Because it has had good business over the past two years, the restaurant expanded."
Why: Good business caused the expansion — a trap answer reversed the cause and effect ("the restaurant expanded, increasing...business").
Workbook — Social Psychology passage
"(7) The debate about the relationship between instinctive responses and those produced by environmental influences, reflective of learned behavior, is not new. (8) The question was discussed as far back as ancient Greece." → "...is not new; the question was discussed as far back as ancient Greece."
Why: Sentence 8 just supports/elaborates sentence 7 — no contrast word needed, a plain semicolon is correct. Trap answers added "however" or "on the other hand," implying a contrast that isn't there.
5Punctuation Rules6 examples

Comma rules show up constantly: setting off an introductory phrase, setting off a nonessential/appositive phrase (needs commas on both sides), and adjective order — plus semicolons joining two complete ideas, and colons introducing a list.

How to solve it: Introductory phrase → comma right where the main sentence starts. Nonessential/appositive phrase (extra info you could delete and still have a complete sentence) → comma on both sides, not just one. Two complete sentences with no conjunction → semicolon, not a comma. A list after a complete sentence → colon.
Workbook — Roses sentence
"White roses represent unity, yellow roses show friendship, and red roses signify love however, many people believe red roses are the most cherished within the genus Rosa." → "...signify love; however, many people believe..."
Why: Two complete sentences joined by "however" need a semicolon right before it, not nothing at all.
Workbook — Piano teacher sentence
"In constant demand as a piano teacher, my brother David has never been busier than he is now."
Why: Introductory descriptive phrase — needs a comma right before the main subject ("my brother David") begins.
Workbook — Jemma sentence
"Unable to decide between a career in law and one in medicine, Jemma combined her two interests and studied forensic medicine."
Why: Same rule, longer introductory phrase — still just needs one comma where the main clause starts.
Workbook — Athletic complex sentence
"The newly constructed athletic complex features a myriad of amenities: multipurpose gymnasiums, an elevated jogging track, squash courts, and exercise studios."
Why: A complete sentence followed by a list needs a colon to introduce it, not just a space or comma.
Workbook — Principal sentence
"Our principal, who met with students at the start of the school year, announced that the field trips this year would be more enjoyable than those we took last year."
Why: "Who met with students..." is nonessential extra info — it needs an opening comma to match the closing comma already there after "year."
Workbook — Miss Adams sentence
"Miss Adams, our swim team coach, chose Sarah to swim the final lap of the three-person relay race at last week's competition in Brooklyn."
Why: "Our swim team coach" is an appositive renaming "Miss Adams" — it opened with a comma but was missing its closing one.
6Precise / Effective Language5 examples

Two common traps here: (a) a vague phrase needs replacing with something concrete and specific, or (b) two near-synonyms are used together, which is just redundant ("difficult and challenging," "buy or purchase").

How to solve it: For vagueness — cross out any option that just rephrases the vague idea with another vague word; the right answer names the actual mechanism or specific detail. For redundancy — if two words in a row mean basically the same thing, the fix is almost always to cut one of them, not replace both.
Exam D · DR14
"In the Whispering Gallery of Grand Central Terminal, people whispering a message in one corner can be clearly heard in the opposite corner, regardless of surrounding noise, because the tiled archways send the sound through the environment." → "...direct the sound waves to move along the curves of the arches."
Why: The fix names the actual physical cause instead of a vague restatement.
Workbook — Tennis sentence
"My sister and I spent nearly all of Saturday evening playing tennis at the park, but we had to stop when it became too difficult and challenging to see the ball." → "...too difficult to see the ball."
Why: "Difficult" and "challenging" are near-synonyms — saying both is redundant.
Workbook — Sunscreen paragraph
(1) Recent experiments seem to indicate that the most effective defense against skin cancer is using sunscreen properly and consistently. (2) Today, 80 percent of Americans buy or purchase sunscreen, but they might need reminders to reapply it every two hours. (3) Melanoma and other types of skin cancer remain a problem... (4) In addition, people might consider switching to a more natural sunscreen...
Why: "Buy or purchase" means the same thing twice.
Workbook — Kaylee sentence
"After graduating from high school, the plan Kaylee has is to attend college in Vermont and to study American literature." → "After graduating from high school, Kaylee plans to attend college in Vermont and to study American literature."
Why: "The plan ___ has is to ___" is wordy and roundabout — a direct verb ("plans to") says the same thing more precisely.
Workbook — Democracy sentence
"The development of democracy's present-day and current principles has required the hard work of many generations of freedom-seeking individuals." → "...democracy's current principles..."
Why: "Present-day" and "current" are redundant synonyms — keep one.
7Transitions5 examples

You're given a blank at the start of a sentence and a word bank (e.g. "However," "For example," "Thus," "Moreover"). Pick the one that correctly signals how the new sentence relates to the one before it.

How to solve it: Name the relationship in plain English first — is the new sentence a contrast (however, nevertheless), a result (thus, consequently, therefore), a specific example (for example), or more of the same idea (moreover, in addition)? Only then look at the options.
Workbook — Social Psychology passage
"(9) There is no question that humans have certain inborn behaviors, or instincts. (10) For example, infants instinctively grasp an object placed in their hands."
Why: Sentence 10 gives one specific instance of the general claim in sentence 9 — a textbook example relationship, not a contrast ("however," "nevertheless") or a summary ("overall").
Workbook — Social Psychology passage
Paragraph 1 describes an instinct-focused theory; paragraph 2 opens with sentence 4: "However, later scholars in the field attributed a significant percentage of human behavior to environmental influences."
Why: Two competing explanations for the same thing is a contrast — even without an obvious "opposite" word, infer it from the shift in whose theory is being described.
Workbook — Havana passage
"(8) Ships from all over the New World unloaded cargo in Havana harbor, from there to be sent on to Spain. (9) However, Spain lost control of Cuba in 1762, when the British captured it during the Seven Years' War."
Why: A positive/established fact, then a negative turn — contrast, not a result ("therefore") or continuation ("consequently").
Workbook — Havana passage
Paragraph 3 (sentences 12–16) covers Havana's history of changing political control. Paragraph 4 opens: "Today, due to its diverse history, Havana has a landscape that can be considered as three distinct areas..."
Why: The landscape described exists BECAUSE of that history — a causal link, not a contrast ("despite," "regardless of"), and "Today" correctly marks the shift from past to present.
Workbook — Music Education paragraph
"(5) We hoped our actions would make the school change its policy and reinstate music programs in the curriculum. (6) However, we hoped in vain."
Why: "Hoped in vain" means the hope failed — a contrast with the hopeful tone just stated, not a result word like "thus" or "therefore."
8Organization / Sentence Order4 examples

A sentence is in the wrong spot — usually because it uses a word ("this," "there," "however," "instead") that only makes sense once some other sentence has already set it up.

How to solve it: Find the "hook" word in the sentence that needs to be moved (a pronoun, "there," "this," a term introduced via abbreviation) and locate exactly which other sentence supplies its missing context. The sentence has to move right next to that one — not just "somewhere earlier/later."
Workbook — Havana passage
Sentence 1 opens the paragraph: "This lively center of all things Cuban looks today much as it did when it was built more than 100 years ago." Sentence 6, at the end of the same paragraph: "The city of Havana stands today as a testament to its turbulent yet glorious history."
Why: Sentence 6 should move to the very beginning — it's the only sentence that actually names Havana, so sentence 1's "This" has nothing to point back to until it moves there.
Workbook — Icebreakers passage
Sentence 10: "The ice is instead crushed under the weight of the ship's bow, which is pushed through by powerful engines." It needed to sit right after the sentence correcting the myth that icebreakers "push" the ice.
Why: "Instead" only makes sense immediately after the myth it's correcting.
Workbook — Jean Piaget passage
Sentence 11: "There, Piaget applied his knowledge of the environment's effect on biological change." It needed to move between sentences 9 and 10, right after the sentence naming "the Sorbonne in Paris."
Why: "There" is a placeholder for a location — it has to immediately follow the sentence that actually names the place.
Workbook — Nizhny Novgorod passage
Sentence 10: "It was the art, however, that generated a storm of controversy in Russian newspapers." It needed to move between sentences 12 and 13, next to the sentences describing the two competing art styles being contrasted.
Why: "However" signals a contrast with something specific just said — the sentence has to sit right after that specific thing.
9Off-Topic Sentence Removal4 examples

One sentence in a paragraph brings up something technically related to the subject but not to the specific point the paragraph is actually making.

How to solve it: State the paragraph's main point in one sentence of your own. Then check each option against that — "does this directly support or explain my one-sentence summary?" The trap sentence is usually true and loosely on-subject, just not relevant to the specific argument being built.
Workbook — Havana passage
Paragraph 3 (sentences 12–16) traces who controlled Havana/Cuba over time — Spain, Britain, the U.S., Cuban independence. Sentence 16 instead says: "Castro's foremost comrade-in-arms was Che Guevara, who was also a doctor and writer."
Why: Related to the same time period, but a biographical tangent, not part of the "who controlled Havana" throughline.
Workbook — Jean Piaget passage
Paragraph 2 (sentences 6–11) is about Piaget's own career. Sentence 10 instead says: "B. F. Skinner and Albert Bandura also studied child development."
Why: True and related to psychology in general, but it shifts focus to other researchers, off the paragraph's actual topic (Piaget specifically).
Workbook — Icebreakers passage
Paragraph 2 (sentences 3–9) is about early ship designs adapting to ice. Sentence 6 instead says: "The winter of 1880–1881 also froze the Elbe river in Germany."
Why: A standalone fact about weather, with no connection to ship-design technology — unlike every other sentence in that paragraph.
Workbook — Crisis Management passage
Paragraph 3 (sentences 11–16) is about Odwalla's response to its E. coli crisis specifically. One sentence instead says: "Odwalla also makes fruit and cereal bars."
Why: True and about Odwalla, but the paragraph is specifically about their crisis response — what else they manufacture doesn't belong.
10Best Concluding / Supporting Sentence4 examples

Pick which sentence should be added to finish a paragraph, or to support/set up a claim just made. The wrong answers are usually too narrow (one more small detail) or contradict what the passage actually argues.

How to solve it: A real conclusion ties together everything the paragraph/passage already said — it shouldn't introduce a brand-new, unsupported idea. A real supporting sentence directly sets up the logic of the sentence that follows it. Eliminate anything that just restates the one sentence right before it, contradicts the passage, or brings up a topic never discussed elsewhere.
Workbook — Winter essay
After three paragraphs each giving one reason winter is the writer's favorite season, the best sentence to end the passage: "For all of these reasons, winter is the season of the year that I enjoy the most." (Trap options included smaller single details, like "Watching the snow fall together gives us time to share the events of the day.")
Why: It explicitly ties together "all of these reasons" — directly restating the passage's thesis — instead of adding one more narrow detail.
Workbook — Crisis Management passage
"(17) A company can easily make a good decision once an emergency is over." → best next sentence: "The real challenge is making them in times of anxiety and panic."
Why: Supplies the missing contrast (easy in hindsight vs. hard in the moment) that logically sets up the next sentence.
Workbook — Icebreakers passage
"(15) Rather, their ability to break through thick ice is due to several unique design features: the bow, the hull, and the propulsion system." → best sentence to come before it: "Icebreakers do not have an immense weight that breaks the ice."
Why: "Rather" signals a correction of a prior claim — the supporting sentence has to set up exactly that misconception for it to correct.
Workbook — Social Psychology passage
After sentence 19 (examples of city overcrowding as an environmental influence on behavior), best concluding sentence: "Since the human experience is never static, the study of social psychology will likely continue to evolve."
Why: It draws the passage's actual conclusion (the field keeps evolving) rather than just restating one example, or — like a trap option — introducing an unsupported claim that some scientists reject the field entirely.
11Style & Tone Matching4 examples

A sentence is too casual (or too flowery) for the rest of the passage. You're asked which revision best matches the register already established — not just which one sounds "more fancy" or "more correct" in isolation.

How to solve it: Read a neighboring sentence to confirm the passage's actual register (plain/factual vs. literary vs. first-person narrative). Cross out any option that's informal/conversational ("you may know," "stuff," "super quick") and any option that overcorrects into purple, exaggerated prose. The right answer is usually the plainest, most direct version that keeps all the original facts and matches the passage's point of view (first person vs. third person).
Workbook — Noctilucent Clouds passage
"We generally agree that a change in atmospheric phenomena may signal pending changes to Earth's climate." → "Scientists generally agree that a change in atmospheric phenomena may signal pending changes to Earth's climate." (A trap option kept "Scientists" but swapped in "a sensational transformation" and "presage imminent changes" — overcorrecting into flowery language.)
Why: Swaps the vague "We" for the objective, specific "Scientists" — matching the rest of the passage's factual register, without over-writing it.
Workbook — Cotton gin / Eli Whitney passage
"The new gadget had a rotating drum with wire spikes that took the fibers people actually wanted and tossed out seeds, which weren't useful anyway." → "The cotton gin consisted of a rotating drum with wire spikes that efficiently caught the desired fibers and left behind the unwanted seeds."
Why: The casual aside "people actually wanted" doesn't fit a passage otherwise written in a historical/technical register.
Workbook — Social Psychology passage
"(17) The field should expand even further." → "(17) In recent years, the field of social psychology has expanded even further."
Why: The sentences right after describe what social psychologists "today also study" — an already-expanded current practice, not a future hope. Match the verb tense to the content, not just the formality.
Workbook — Music Education paragraph
"(4) Those comments made them feel that we were really effecting our school atmosphere." → "(4) Those comments made us feel that we were really affecting our school atmosphere."
Why: The passage is first-person throughout ("My friends and I") so "us" is correct, not "them" — and "affecting" (influencing) is the right word, not "effecting" (bringing about). Style matching isn't just about formality — it includes point of view and word choice consistency too.
12Commonly Confused Words & Word Forms5 examples

A grab-bag of small, specific mix-ups that show up again and again: possessive vs. contraction ("its" vs. "it's"), "who" vs. "that" for people, irregular past participles ("rang" vs. "rung"), and comparative vs. superlative ("better" vs. "best").

How to solve it: These aren't really about logic — they're about recognizing five or six specific, recurring mix-ups. its/it's: "it's" always means "it is" — if you can't substitute "it is" into the sentence, it should be "its." who/that: use "who" for people, "that" for things. Comparative vs. superlative: comparing exactly 2 things → "-er"/"more" (better, more); comparing 3+ things → "-est"/"most" (best, most). Irregular verbs: past tense ("rang," "swam," "began") vs. the form used with "has/have/was/were" (rung, swum, begun) are NOT interchangeable.
Workbook — Statue sentence
"Although the statue was intended, in ancient times, to tower above the landscape, it's presence is now dominated by the enormous skyscrapers that came with the city's modernization." → "...its presence is now dominated..."
Why: Try the substitution: "it is presence" doesn't make sense, so it needs the possessive "its" (no apostrophe).
Workbook — Sprinter sentence
"Between Hugo and Kai, Hugo is the best sprinter; however, Kai is better at the long jump." → "...the better sprinter; however, Kai is better at the long jump."
Why: Only two people are being compared ("Between Hugo and Kai") — that calls for the comparative "better," not the superlative "best" (reserved for 3+).
Workbook — Dog show paragraph
(1) The winners of the "Best of Breed" cup... are selected by a panel of breeders from the American Kennel Club. (2) Since 1884, this organization has promoted responsible dog ownership... (3) Each year, it hosts tens of thousands of events, including dog shows. (4) The American Kennel Club even reunites lost pets with their owners and offers scholarships to the brighter veterinary students.
Why: The opposite mistake from the sprinter example — selecting among many students (3+) needs the superlative "brightest," not the comparative "brighter."
Workbook — Liberty Bell paragraph
(1) The Liberty Bell, an enormous instrument that was eventually rang during the American Revolution, received its first small crack as it was being tested. (2) When the Declaration of Independence had its first public proclamation, the people of Philadelphia heard this bell ring out. (3) The Liberty Bell's crack grew much larger when it was rung on Washington's birthday in 1846...
Why: After "was/were" (passive voice), irregular verbs need their participle form — "rung," not the simple past "rang" (you'd say "it rang" but "it was rung" — notice sentence 3 already gets this right).
Workbook — Women's equality paragraph
(1) Women have seen improved conditions regarding social and political equality over the years. (2) For example, some companies offer a "Take Your Daughter to Work Day"...
